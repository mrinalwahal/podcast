## The spine (6–8 anchor questions; control → alternative → honesty)
1. [from the story] …so whose money is it, really — yours, or the state's?
2. Beyond the hidden tax from last time — how is *controlling* money used as power over people? *(legal tender; demonetisation)*
3. A "digital rupee" the state can program, watch and switch off. Steelman the case for it first (convenience, inclusion, less black money) — then, what could it enable? *(surveillance, expiry, exclusion)*
4. So if state money can be taxed, frozen and cancelled, what's the alternative — money it can't debase or seize? *(gold, competing currencies, Bitcoin)*
5. "Separation of money and state" — what would that even look like in India? *(Hayek's competing currencies; Bitcoin as escape hatch)*
6. Be straight: what breaks with each option? *(gold: deflation/rigidity, seized in 1933; Bitcoin: volatility, adoption; free banking: panics; the poor's reliance on cash + welfare)*
7. Steelman the state once more — a *legitimate* role in money, and where's the line? Does that role require a *monopoly*?
8. **Close:** if you could take one power over money away from the state, which — and why?

## Steelman first, then judge
Make the state/CBDC case strong (a monopoly lowers transaction costs; lender of last resort; a CBDC could bank the unbanked, cut cash costs, curb evasion) — *then* push: does any of that require the power to **surveil, program, and freeze** ordinary people's money? Convenience and control ride the same rails.

## Concede our own side
Gold: deflation, rigidity, seizable (1933). Bitcoin: volatility, adoption/regression, maturing. Free banking: real 19th-century panics. Hardest for India: a poor, informal, cash-reliant population that also leans on state welfare rails — reckon with it, don't wave it away.

## Takeaway to steer toward
*"Money the state controls is money that can be taxed, frozen, watched, or cancelled. The real question isn't how much inflation to allow — it's why the state gets to control money at all."*
